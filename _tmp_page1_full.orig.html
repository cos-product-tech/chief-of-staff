Career track · Management · Six levels, Team Lead through Vice President

Engineering Managers at Priority Commerce lead the teams that build technology for moving money. A manager's product is the team: its delivery, its health, its standards, and the growth of every engineer on it. Management is a change of craft, not a promotion — the IC and management tracks are parallel and equally valued, movement between them at equivalent levels is normal career mobility in both directions (see Career Mobility), and a Staff engineer is not "below" the manager they work with. A manager's value is measured by what their team accomplishes that it couldn't have without them; the moment a team succeeds because of its manager's heroics rather than its own capability, the manager is failing at the actual job.

We are outcome-driven, never task-oriented. Every person on this team can name the business value of what they're building: the customer it serves, the outcome it should move, and how we'll know it worked. Tasks closed, features shipped, and hours worked are outputs — we measure ourselves by outcomes: customer experience and business results. Shipping is the beginning of the evidence, not the end of the job.

Managers own four bars. The hiring bar — every hire either raises the team's level or lowers it, and managers are accountable for which. The performance bar — growth is celebrated, and underperformance is addressed directly and early; avoiding the hard conversation is choosing the team's decline. The culture bar — psychological safety, honest debate, and quality-is-everyone's-job are team properties managers deliberately build, not hope for. And the measurement bar — you can't coach what you can't see: individuals are measured against their role card, with evidence — never activity metrics; teams are measured with delivery data through the company's engineering management platform — outcomes first, paired system signals beneath them (a speed signal only counts when its quality pair held), activity as diagnostics. A number that improved while its outcome didn't is a flag, not a win. Managers also lead the AI-native transformation where it lives: building teams where engineers direct agents, own outcomes end-to-end, and treat role boundaries as craft specialties, not fences.

Every expectation in these cards reads through a leadership lens: the evidence is what your teams do because of you, not what you do personally. And every level of this track is technical. The senior technical bar set at Manager never lapses as you rise — what grows is the number of rooms it must hold in — until a leader can represent their scope's technical direction alone, faithfully enough that their technical partner would co-sign what they committed. Dividing and conquering is the design; dividing the direction is the failure.

How levels and the five capability areas work: Levels and Capability Areas for how the system works.

The five capability areas — what each block means

Craft — excellence in the track's discipline: for managers, the craft of building and running teams — hiring, feedback, performance, conditions.

Impact — customer and business outcomes within your actual scope; delivery others can plan on.

Ownership — operational excellence for what you run: reliability, security, quality; prevention, preparation, and resilience — your scope doesn't need heroes.

Influence — communication (especially written), collaboration, growing people and leaders; raising the standards around you.

Judgment — decision quality: tradeoffs, risk, knowing when to decide, escalate, or push back; decisions that age well.

For your manager — using these cards in coaching and assessment

The first sentence of each capability block is the expectation; everything after it tells you what evidence looks like. Score the area as a whole: Below means the block's anchor — its first sentence — is not yet a fair description of this person's sustained behavior, even after accounting for the opportunities they actually had. At means the block, read together, fairly describes their sustained behavior — name any clause that isn't yet true in your evidence notes. Above means the next card's block is the fairer description, evidenced. Matching a clause of the next card is not Above — Above means the next block, read whole, is the fairer description of your sustained behavior. These are the per-area diagnostic reads; the official annual rating uses Priority Commerce's company rating scale, decided at calibration — see Engineering Talent Processes.

Nobody is scored down for opportunities the organization never gave them — note the constraint, then create the opportunity before the next cycle. Prevention counts as evidence: the crisis that never happened, the team that runs well even when its leader is away. See Engineering Talent Processes for the full assessment guide. Team Lead, Engineering

Your team's people, delivery, and growth are yours — and you still write the code that keeps you honest.

What a Team Lead typically owns: one team, as a player-coach: full ownership of the people side — hiring, performance, and every engineer's growth — while contributing technically at roughly half their time, with their personal work deliberately kept off the sprint's critical path. The Team Lead is the team's people leader; the team's senior engineer (typically Staff or above) is its technical leader — two interlocking roles. This role is a two-way door by design: entering from Senior, returning to Senior, or advancing to Manager are all normal outcomes, and trying the role is not a one-way commitment.

What's new vs. Senior Software Engineer (a lateral move): accountability for people, not just systems — engineers' growth is your deliverable, hiring and performance decisions are yours, and feedback is a job duty, not a favor. Not yet expected (that's Manager): management as the entire job; holding the technical bar without writing the code.

Craft — The people side of the team — hiring, feedback, performance, growth — is run as a craft, not a side duty. Hires engineers who raise the team's level and can show which hires did; gives feedback that is regular, specific, and acted on; assesses each report honestly against their role card. Runs the team's operating rhythm — planning, review, retro — so work flows without them pushing every piece. Models AI-assisted engineering rather than merely permitting it.

Impact — The team delivers more predictably with them than before them; sprint commitments are sized honestly and misses get examined, not excused — with the data open: the team runs on real signals, outcomes first, system health beside them, activity as diagnostics. In partnership with their Product and Design peers, every engineer on the team can articulate the customer problem and the outcomes the team is trying to move.

Ownership — Performance issues get addressed before they're crises. The team's on-call is humane and doesn't depend on one person; during team-level critical moments, coordinates from the people-leader seat — owns the response and ensures it's led well — while the engineers closest to the code lead the technical work.

Influence — Engineers cite the Team Lead's feedback and coaching as the reason they grew. Operates as a real partnership with the team's senior engineer — people leadership and technical leadership interlocking, neither overriding the other.

Judgment — Knows which decisions are theirs (people, priorities, process) and which belong to the team's technical leader or beyond; escalates architecture decisions past the team's surface to their owners rather than settling them by title.

You're succeeding when: the team delivers more predictably on your watch, your hires raised its level, a performance issue got addressed early and turned around (or resolved honestly), engineers name your coaching in their growth — and you've discovered you care more about the team's output than your own.

Growth path → Manager, Engineering — or back to the IC track, a normal move in both directions

Manager, Engineering

One team (or a few) — its delivery, its people, its standards — performs because you own all of it, full time.

What a Manager typically owns: one to several teams, as a full-time craft; no manager reports. The team's performance is the manager's contribution — the leverage flows through judgment, hiring, coaching, standards, and the conditions they create, not their own commits. They can still pick up the keyboard when the work genuinely calls for it, but don't lean on it as their contribution.

What's new vs. Team Lead: management as the whole job; holding a senior technical bar in any conversation — a design review, an incident, a roadmap debate — without writing the code. Not yet expected (that's Senior Manager): leading through other managers.

Craft — Hiring, feedback, and performance management operate as a system, not events: the bar is consistent, the assessments are honest, and underperformance is addressed early and directly. Can interrogate a design and hold the technical bar in any conversation — and the team's senior engineer would say so. AI-native practice is a team standard with expectations, not an encouraged option.

Impact — The team delivers predictably and sustainably; commitments to product, design, and business partners hold, and they're trusted because they keep holding. The team's outcomes — the metrics it exists to move — move on the manager's watch, in partnership with their Product and Design peers. The team is instrumented through the company's engineering management platform — the outcomes it exists to move, the paired delivery-and-quality signals beneath them (a speed signal only counts when its quality pair held), and the activity beneath those — and improves on what it measures, with the fix nameable. A metric that improved while its outcome didn't is a flag, not a win.

Ownership — Owns the team's operational health: ensures SLO coverage exists for the team's services and is honored, deploy frequency and lead time improve, on-call stays humane, and the team never ships untested work downstream as someone else's problem. During high-pressure moments, orchestrates the response — outward communication, decision-making, protecting the team's ability to recover — owns the response and ensures it's led well. They actively identify the team's single points of failure — including themselves — and close them: named shadows, deliberate rotation, documented knowledge.

Influence — Engineers grow into and beyond their levels. Some grow past the manager, into roles the manager championed. Measured on the team's outcomes alongside the team's senior engineer, each through their own lever: the manager owns delivery, people, and conditions; the senior engineer owns technical direction — respected and amplified, never overridden.

Judgment — Knows when to protect the team and when to push it; when to pick up the keyboard and when doing so would steal the growth moment; which battles to escalate and which to absorb. Debt and platform work get real roadmap space because the manager makes the tradeoff explicit rather than silently mortgaging the future.

You're succeeding when: commitments hold quarter after quarter, your hires raise the bar, an underperformance case was handled early and fairly, engineers you developed now operate a level up — and no part of the team's operation depends on a single head — including yours.

Growth path → Senior Manager, Engineering

Senior Manager, Engineering

Multiple teams perform consistently — and the leaders under you grow — because you lead leaders as well as engineers.

What a Senior Manager typically owns: multiple teams within an area — managing other leaders (Team Leads and Managers), often while running a team directly. The newly-yours craft is indirection: affecting outcomes through leaders you develop rather than presence — and inconsistency between your teams is now your problem.

What's new vs. Manager: a second mode of leadership — coaching, delegation, and standards through other managers; developing leaders as a multiplier. Not yet expected (that's Director): owning a full workstream's outcomes and leadership structure end-to-end.

Craft — Develops the Managers and Team Leads under them as an active craft — those leaders grow measurably and say why. Keeps hiring and performance bars consistent across teams: no leader under them grades differently than they would. The senior technical bar carries across every team they lead: they can interrogate any team's design without the local senior engineer in the room, and technical standards don't drift between teams because the Senior Manager can tell the difference. AI-native practice is consistent across their teams — a standard held, not a per-team choice. Any team run directly is held to the full Manager standard.

Impact — Delivery and outcomes are consistent across teams, not lumpy; cross-team dependencies get sequenced so multi-team commitments hold; the area's goals ladder up coherently with their Product and Design peers — and every team in the area can name the customer outcomes its work serves. Measurement is consistent across teams — same signals, same pairings, no leader under them measuring differently or optimizing a number at its outcome's expense — and rollups distinguish drift from healthy variation.

Ownership — Structural friction between teams gets engineered away, not mediated repeatedly. They actively identify the area's single points of failure — including themselves — and close them: named shadows, deliberate rotation, documented knowledge. During cross-team critical moments, owns the response and ensures it's led well.

Influence — The leaders under them carry the standard into rooms the Senior Manager never enters — the evidence is what those leaders now handle alone. Measured on the area's outcomes alongside the senior engineers across its teams, each through their own lever, held consistently.

Judgment — Reads which team needs help versus which needs space; moves capacity and attention to where risk concentrates; knows when inconsistency between teams is healthy local variation and when it's drift that will compound.

You're succeeding when: your teams' performance is consistent rather than dependent on which one you're watching, the leaders under you cite you as the reason they grew, any team you run directly performs at least as well as those led by your reports, and cross-team commitments hold.

Growth path → Director, Engineering

Director, Engineering

A workstream's outcomes, organization, and engineering health are yours — and the structure you built delivers them.

What a Director typically owns: a full workstream: its delivery, its leadership structure, its engineering health, and its partnership with product strategy. Organizational design becomes the craft — building the structure of teams, leaders, and processes that lets the workstream deliver without depending on any individual, including the Director. Most of the leverage flows through the leadership team they build — and they may also run a team or engineers directly when the moment calls for it, held to the full Manager standard. The workstream's results are theirs in the eyes of the business.

What's new vs. Senior Manager: the results are yours in the business's eyes; org design — structure, roles, succession — is a primary work product; investment tradeoffs join delivery as the job. Not yet expected (that's Senior Director): multiple workstreams and portfolio-level tradeoffs between them.

Craft — Designs the organization deliberately: structure, roles, and succession as work products, revised when structure is the bottleneck. Builds and develops the Manager and Senior Manager bench; the leadership team can fill the Director's gaps — and it shows when tested: absences, escalations, and hard quarters get covered without the Director in the room.

Impact — The workstream delivers what the business plans on; commitments to partners, stakeholders, and customers hold, and quarterly attainment is measured and defended with the data at the executive table. With their Product and Design peers, the workstream's goals are business commitments defended together at the executive level, in business language — and AI-native capability is a workstream-wide expectation with evidence behind it.

Ownership — Allocates engineering investment across the company's strategic pillars — explicit, evidenced in the engineering management platform rather than asserted, defended in business terms, revisited quarterly; the workstream never mortgages its future silently. During the workstream's highest-stakes moments, owns the response and ensures it's led well. They actively identify the workstream's single points of failure — including themselves — and close them: named shadows, deliberate rotation, documented knowledge.

Influence — The partnership with the workstream's Principal SWE is the canonical peer relationship, and it's generative, not territorial: the Director owns delivery, people, organization, and measurement; the Principal owns technical direction and architecture health; both are measured on the workstream's outcomes through their own lever, and unresolved disagreement escalates to the lowest shared leader rather than festering. The partnership can also divide and conquer: the Director carries the workstream's technical direction alone when needed — in front of the business, a customer, or a crisis — faithfully enough that the Principal would co-sign every commitment made in their absence. And the workstream's quality leaders — the Manager, QA Engineering and the Principal QAE and Principal SDET — plan with the Director, not around them.

Judgment — Organizational calls age well: the structure built two years ago is why today's scale works. Knows when a team needs a leader change, when a structure needs redesign, and when the honest answer to the business is no.

You're succeeding when: the workstream delivers what the business plans on, the Principal partnership is generative, the leadership bench fills your gaps when tested, executives treat the workstream as a strategic partner defended by its own leader — and no part of the workstream's operation depends on a single head — including yours.

Growth path → Senior Director, Engineering

Senior Director, Engineering

Multiple workstreams deliver consistently — and their Directors grow — because you built organizations that work at scale.

What a Senior Director typically owns: multiple workstreams and the layered leadership organizations that run them. The craft is scale: making excellence consistent across organizations they can't personally inspect — through the leaders they develop, the structures they design, and the standards they hold. The levers are almost entirely indirect: who you hire, who you grow, what you measure, what you tolerate, what structure you put in place.

What's new vs. Director: workstreams, plural — including their tradeoffs against each other; succession as a system, not a plan. Not yet expected (that's Vice President): company-level engineering strategy and economics; executive peerships at company scope.

Craft — Develops Directors and Senior Managers; succession options are real at every leadership level and have been exercised. Changes organizational structure when it's the bottleneck — and can point to the change and what it unlocked.

Impact — Multi-workstream initiatives land when promised; systemic predictability problems get organizational fixes, not repeated escalations. Investment shifts across areas follow strategy, not inertia or volume — and the AI-native transformation has organizational momentum across their workstreams.

Ownership — The areas converge rather than drift — and convergence is verified by measurement, not reporting: outcome, system, and allocation signals are comparable across workstreams, and investment follows what they show. Reliability posture, quality standards, and delivery health are consistent across organizations. Major-incident learnings propagate across workstreams — the same class doesn't recur in a second organization. They actively identify the areas' single points of failure — including themselves — and close them: named shadows, deliberate rotation, documented knowledge. During multi-workstream critical moments, owns the response and ensures it's led well.

Influence — Directors grow under them and say why. Sustains multi-quarter backing for architecture, modernization, and platform investments — the bets they champion don't lose funding the moment they get hard. Partners with the Principal Software Engineers across their workstreams and with the Principal Software Architects, so workstream strategies and platform direction stay aligned in both directions — and can hold the technical conversation in any of their workstreams without the Principal in the room.

Judgment — Portfolio calls age well: which area to invest in, which to hold, which structure to change, which leader to bet on. Distinguishes healthy local variation from drift at organizational scale.

You're succeeding when: your areas' outcomes and engineering health are consistently strong regardless of where you're looking, Directors grow under you and succession is real, cross-area initiatives land, and the organizations converge rather than drift.

Growth path → Vice President, Engineering

Vice President, Engineering

Engineering delivers the company's strategy — reliably, at scale, and increasingly AI-natively — because you built the organization that does it.

What a Vice President typically owns: a major portion of Priority Commerce's engineering organization, at executive scope. The craft is alignment at company scale: connecting engineering strategy to business strategy, designing the organization that executes it, and building the leadership team that makes the whole system work without heroics. Most of the leverage is institutional — the leaders developed, the systems designed, the standards held, the strategy authored.

What's new vs. Senior Director: the company, not just the platform — engineering economics as a first-class accountability, executive peer relationships across the business, and decisions measured in years. This is the track's most senior level.

Craft — Sets engineering strategy with the CTO and executive peers, and the organization executes it. Owns organizational design at scale: the organization survives the loss of any leader — including them — because the structure and pipeline were built for it. Develops Senior Directors and Directors; the leadership pipeline produces leaders rather than depleting them.

Impact — Engineering's commitments to the company hold, and the business's confidence in engineering is earned on evidence, not asserted. Owns engineering economics at company scale — investment allocation across the strategic pillars, unit costs, build-vs-buy at scale — and the business case for engineering investment wins where it should. The measurement culture itself is an executive accountability: outcome-anchored, paired, never individual — everywhere.

Ownership — The organization's operational posture is an executive accountability: reliability, security, and incident culture are measured, improving, and blameless. During the company's highest-stakes engineering moments, owns the response and ensures it's led well.

Influence — A real business partner to executive peers across product, design, and the business — engineering isn't a translation layer between the business and the technical work; it's inseparable from the business. Sponsors the AI-native transformation as an executive priority with budget, structure, and visibility — and the evidence that it's underway.

Judgment — Bets measured in years age well: the platform investments, organizational designs, and leadership choices made on their watch are why the company can do what it does now. Knows what the organization should stop doing, and says so with the same conviction as what it should start.

You're succeeding when: engineering's commitments to the company hold, the leadership pipeline produces, the transformation is visibly real rather than performed, the company trusts engineering — and the organization would survive any leadership change, including yours.
