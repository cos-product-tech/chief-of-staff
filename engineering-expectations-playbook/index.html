Engineering Expectations — The Playbook

The Priority Commerce Engineering Expectations are what excellent engineering looks like at every level — public, written down, and behind hiring, assessment, development, and promotion alike. This page is the whole framework as a guided walk, built for the person the framework is about — you, the engineer: eight plays, in order, each giving you the point in one breath, the page to open, and why it matters. Walk it yourself in fifteen minutes — and the same eight plays work word-for-word whenever you're the one introducing the framework to someone else.

Read this first — how to hold this framework. Everything here is a map of what excellent looks like, written down so nobody has to guess. Read it for its intent, and bring your judgment with you — a person applying the spirit imperfectly beats one following the letter blindly, every time. And hold one thing clearly from the start: the expectations are not a checklist that sums to a promotion. Evidence is weighed — for quality, for how long it's been sustained, for the outcomes it produced — never counted. Every official rating and every promotion is decided at calibration, where evidence is compared across the entire engineering organization, precisely so that the same bar means the same thing on every team. What this framework promises is not an outcome; it's something better: a bar you can see, feedback in a language you share, no surprises at review time, and a written route forward whenever the answer is "not yet." The bar is public. Clearing it is still craft.

The framework in sixty seconds

01Every role has a public card

Every level of every track has a role card: five capability areas — Craft, Impact, Ownership, Influence, Judgment — each a written expectation with what evidence looks like. Yours is public. So is everyone else's, including your leaders'.

02One card, five uses

The same card is the job description, the interview standard, the assessment line, the development plan (read one card up — that's the assignment), and the promotion criterion. One source of truth; no translation between what's coached and what's measured.

03Outcomes, not activity

We are outcome-driven, never task-oriented: everyone can name the customer their work serves and the result it should move. Shipping is the beginning of the evidence, not the end of the job.

The introduction — eight plays, in order

This is the walk. Each play gives you the point in one breath, the page to open, and the why behind it. Introducing the framework to someone else? The point, said in your own voice, is your script — put the open link on screen, and the why is there for when they ask. Fifteen minutes covers all eight; each play stands alone.

Play 1 · Start at the front door THE WHOLE SYSTEM, ONE PAGE
THE POINT"Every expectation for every level of every engineering role at Priority Commerce is written down and public — including mine, and including our leaders'. This is the front door; everything else hangs off this page."
OPENPriority Commerce Engineering Expectations — scroll it slowly; the point is that it all exists.
WHYTransparency is the foundation everything else stands on: you can't be measured against a bar you can't see, and here, nobody is. It's a shared operating contract — clear expectations and decision rights for every role, shared accountability for every outcome.
Play 2 · Open your card — then read one card up THE PERSONAL HOOK
THE POINT"Find your role and level. That card is your job, in writing. Now read the card one level up — that's not a wish list, that's your development plan, and reading it is the assignment."
OPENRole Cards — open the person's actual track: Software Engineering, Architecture, QA Engineering, SDET, Engineering Management, or QAE Management.
WHYThe framework becomes real the moment someone reads their own name's worth of it. This is the play that converts skeptics — let them sit with their card for a minute before moving on.
Play 3 · How a card is read FIVE AREAS, ONE RULE
THE POINT"Five areas — Craft, Impact, Ownership, Influence, Judgment. The first sentence of each block is the expectation; everything after it shows what evidence looks like. A read is Below, At, or Above — about sustained behavior, never a single quarter, good or bad — and nobody is scored down for opportunities they were never given. One rule keeps it honest: matching a single clause of the next card isn't Above — Above means the next card's whole block describes you better, evidenced."
OPENLevels and Capability Areas — the reading rules, published once, applied everywhere.
WHYThis one rule is what makes feedback, assessment, and promotion speak the same language. When anyone says "that's the Senior Craft expectation," everyone can look at the same sentence.
Play 4 · Outcomes are half of everything THE DOCTRINE
THE POINT"Capability is half the story. The other half is delivery: the business outcomes you and your team committed to — revenue, efficiency, customer experience, adoption, reliability, your initiatives' KPIs. Shipping is the beginning of the evidence, not the end of the job."
OPENThe top of any track page — e.g., Software Engineering — the outcome doctrine sits at the top of every one, in the same words.
WHYThis is what separates the framework from a skills rubric: it's wired to what the business needs delivered, and it makes on-time delivery of committed outcomes visible, valued, and career-relevant.
Play 5 · The evidence note — the one habit to start today HIGHEST-LEVERAGE HABIT
THE POINT"One dated line, every time card-level evidence happens — the design doc that got used, the incident class that stopped happening, the person you grew. Newest first. Nobody remembers March when the review arrives in January; this note is what makes your year visible instead of recency-biased. Keep your own — your manager keeps a separate one on you, and where the two agree, the evidence corroborates itself."
OPENAssembling Evidence Notes — and the Evidence Note template (File → Make a copy).
WHYKeeping this note is the single highest-leverage career habit the framework rewards — and it's the piece anyone can start within five minutes of this introduction.
Play 6 · How assessment actually works TWO LAYERS, NO SURPRISES
THE POINT"Once a year, in Q1, a written Capability Assessment looks back at the previous year: your five areas read against your card, with evidence — and the official rating on the company's 1-to-5 scale. You already know this scale: it's the one from your 15Five self review — Below Expectations, Developing, Consistently Effective, Highly Effective, Exceptional. You rate yourself on it, your manager rates you on it, and calibration decides the final — from your five card reads, the outcomes you delivered, and comparison across the whole organization. If anything in it surprises you, the process failed upstream — the rhythm all year is exactly so that it never does."
OPENEngineering Talent Processes — the Capability Assessment — the two layers, and the company scale verbatim.
WHYCalibration is the fairness mechanism, not a black box: it exists so the same evidence earns the same result on every team.
Play 7 · Promotion — demonstrated, not projected THE QUESTION
THE POINT"Every promotion answers one question: what evidence shows this person is already operating at the next level, sustained — with their committed business outcomes delivered? Readiness can be raised at any quarterly calibration. And a 'not yet' comes with a written gap map naming exactly what evidence would change the answer — it's a route, never a rejection."
OPENAssembling a Promotion Packet — and Career Mobility for moves between tracks, both directions, destigmatized.
WHY"Already operating at" kills the two things that poison promotion systems: tenure arguments and potential arguments. And the gap map means nobody ever leaves calibration without a map home.
Play 8 · The operating system around you HOW WE WORK, MEASURED HONESTLY
THE POINT"The cards reference how we work — the quality trio where an escaped defect is the team's failure and never a role's, written decisions with an afterlife, the duty to close single points of failure including yourself, and AI-native practice with judgment. Teams are measured on these practices quarterly — and there's a firewall: no team score is ever evidence about a person, in either direction."
OPENEngineering Operating System — the Team EMM — skim the headings on the way; land on the three firewalls.
WHYThe firewalls are the trust play: individuals are assessed against their card with evidence, teams are measured with delivery data, and the two never mix. A strong engineer on a struggling team is exactly who this system must recognize — and it's built to.

The vocabulary — ten terms that unlock everything

Role card — your level's five written capability expectations. The unit of the whole system: JD, interview standard, assessment line, development plan, promotion criterion.
The anchor — the first sentence of each capability block. It's the expectation; the rest of the block is what evidence looks like.
Below / At / Above — the diagnostic read per area, against sustained behavior. Above means the next card's whole block describes you better — evidenced.
Evidence note — the running, dated record (newest first). You keep one on yourself; your manager keeps a separate one on you; corroboration happens where they agree.
Calibration — the quarterly panel that holds one standard across the organization. Final ratings and promotions are decided there, on written evidence.
Gap map — what a "not yet" produces: the blocks the evidence doesn't yet fairly meet, and what evidence would change that. A route, never a rejection.
The two-lever split — the manager owns delivery, people, conditions; the senior technical leader owns technical direction. Same outcomes, different levers, neither overrides the other.
The quality trio — SE + QAE + SDET on one problem, one scoreboard, no handoffs. Quality is everyone's job; an escaped defect is the team's failure, never a role's.
The single-point-of-failure duty — actively find what only one head holds — including yours — and close it: named shadows, rotation, documented knowledge.
Team EMM — the quarterly Team Engineering Maturity Model assessment, firewalled absolutely from individual assessment: no team score is ever evidence about a person.
