Office of the CTO · Modernizing the tech delivery engine · Starting with Merchant Experience

Make Merchant Experience fast and predictable
on the commitments it makes to the business

This is broader than AI-native development. It is about modernizing how the team plans, builds, tests, and ships: operating rhythms, Jellyfish data turned into action, process and organizational drag, and whatever is slowing the team down, people, process, tools, silos, complexity, or infrastructure. AI-native PDLC is one lever among several.

Problem statement
The Merchant Experience team is not fast enough and cannot predictably deliver on the commitments it promised the business.
What we will find out first
Where the time actually goes, and whether the team understands the business, the customer, the problems it is solving, and the KPIs that would show an outcome was achieved, or whether it is running as a feature factory.
How we will know
A Jellyfish dashboard on six dimensions, baselined before anything changes and re-baselined after four sprints of running the changes. The gate is simple: commitments shipped on the date promised.

Scope stay focused on the problem statement

In scope

  • Operating rhythms: how work enters, how commitments are made and against what capacity, how status flows to the business.
  • Jellyfish data used to find and act on specific drag.
  • Process and organizational burden: approvals, handoffs, environments, provisioning, regression length, silos between engineering, QA, product, design, and the business.
  • Whether the team understands the business, the customer, the problem, and the KPI behind each commitment, and making sure every commitment carries that context from the day it is made.
  • How the team responds to business asks: capacity checks, trades, and how the business hears about changes. We change the team's side of that exchange, not the business's.
  • AI-native PDLC, paired in by the Innovation Lab, where it removes observed friction.

Out of scope, deliberately

  • Turning Merchant Experience into a product-led team. At Priority Commerce our business partners drive product; changing that is a longer transformation than this problem needs, and it would take too long.
  • Reorganizing the team or changing its leaders as a lever in this window. If structure turns out to be the constraint, that goes to the CTO as a recommendation.
  • Redesigning the sprint and regression cadence. Shortening regression through automation and AI is in scope; replacing the cadence is not.
  • Anything that needs business partners to change how they work. Those ideas go to a parking lot for the CTO, not into this plan.

Business, customer, problem
  • Who is the customer for the last thing you shipped, and what changed for them?
  • What business result was it meant to move? How would we know it did?
  • Who decides what the team works on, and how does it reach you?
Commitments and planning
  • When the team commits a date, what is it based on? Who checks capacity?
  • How often does work get added mid-sprint, and what gets dropped when it does?
  • When a date slips, when does the business find out, and from whom?
Where the time goes
  • Walk me through the last item you finished, from pickup to production. Where did it wait?
  • What are your three biggest time sinks? People, process, tools, silos, complexity, environments, provisioning, approvals.
  • Which meetings, documents, or status updates could disappear tomorrow without anyone noticing?
Quality and regression
  • What does the two-week regression window actually catch? What would you cut from it?
  • Where do defects come from, and how long until they're fixed?
  • What breaks in production, and why does it get through?
Tools and AI today
  • What do you already use AI for? Where has it helped, and where has it wasted your time?
  • Which tool or system do you fight with most?
If you could change one thing
  • What would you change first, and what stops you from changing it?
  • What would make you trust a date you give the business?

Phases each with an exit test

Phase 1 · Oct 13–23

Prepare

  • Eric hosts Bart, Amrita, Taylor, Lilly, and Steven to review the preliminary plan and sharpen it.
  • Write the interview script above; run nine 30-minute interviews by Friday, Oct 23.
  • Decide the KPIs we will track on the six dimensions and the gate.
  • Eric builds the Jellyfish dashboard for those KPIs and baselines the team before anything changes; review it with the core team, then with everyone we interview: Erin, Kyle, Quan, Andrew, Mamatha, Alex, Ryan, Michael, and Earnest.
ExitInterviews done · KPIs chosen · dashboard live and baselined · plan reviewed
Phase 2 · Oct 26 – Nov 20

Distill, embed, observe

  • Distill the interviews into a ranked list of what slows the team down and what it doesn't understand about the business, customer, problem, or KPIs.
  • Eric, Amrita, and Lilly in every ritual alongside Alex for one full cycle: Sprint 22 from planning through development, regression and QA, and its Nov 22 release.
  • Trace two or three real items end to end in Jira and Jellyfish, from pickup through development, review, QA, regression, and release, and record how long each waited at every step and why. Compare what people said in interviews with what the Jellyfish benchmark dashboard and the observation show.
  • Watch commitments being made: against what capacity, with what information, and how the business hears about changes.
ExitDrag ranked by cost with evidence from interviews, data, and observation · the two or three biggest constraints named
Phase 3 · Nov 23 – Dec 4

Pair and decide

  • Amrita pairs with each engineer on the team, one by one, on AI-native PDLC; Bart pairs with Erin from Product; Steven pairs with each QA Engineer. Everyone has tried it on their own work before Dec 7.
  • Decide the ideas we will implement against the problem statement: no more than three or four, each aimed at a named constraint, at least one aimed at the regression window. Right-sizing commitments into smaller batches is a candidate from day one.
  • Fix the commitment habit as one of them: capacity known before planning, no mid-sprint additions without a trade, every commitment written with the customer, the problem, and the KPI it is meant to move, and a weekly one-line note to business partners on what was committed, what shipped, and what moved.
ExitChanges chosen and written down · every engineer paired · go / no-go for Dec 7
Phase 4 · Dec 7 – Feb 12

Run the changes, then re-baseline

  • Changes go live at the Sprint 25 boundary and run for four development sprints: 25, 26, 1, and 2, holidays included. Amrita, Bart, and Steven embedded through Sprint 26 at minimum.
  • Alex, as the Principal, carries the changes inside the team.
  • Expect a dip in Sprint 25. Continue unless it persists through Sprint 26.
  • Dec 18 checkpoint after the first sprint. Releases built with the changes ship Jan 1, Jan 15, Jan 29, and Feb 12.
  • For every commitment shipped, record the KPI it was meant to move; at the re-baseline, report which ones moved.
  • Feb 12: re-baseline the dashboard against Phase 1 and decide: continue, adjust, or stop; and whether the next team starts.
Exit · Feb 12 re-baselineFour sprints and four releases read as one block against the baseline · a written account of what changed and why · next-team decision made

Risks and decision rules named now so no one argues later

Scope creep into a product transformation. Everything we find will tempt us to fix how product is driven.If a change needs business partners to work differently, it goes to the CTO parking lot. This plan changes what the team controls, including the context every commitment carries.
Output without outcome. Shipping on the promised date is the gate, but a date kept on a feature that moved nothing is still a feature factory.Every commitment carries the KPI it is meant to move; the re-baseline reports which ones moved, not just which ones shipped.
Interviews that say what people think we want to hear. Nine people, several of them each other's managers.One on one, thirty minutes, synthesized without attribution. Observation in Phase 2 checks what was said against what happens.
Fixing symptoms instead of the constraint. The first visible problem is rarely the biggest one.The end-to-end traces and the Jellyfish benchmark dashboard decide, not the loudest interview. No more than three or four changes, each tied to a named constraint.
Data nobody trusts. Q3 definitions were still moving; a disputed baseline sinks the re-baseline.Dashboard and definitions agreed before Oct 26 with everyone we interview: Erin, Kyle, Quan, Andrew, Mamatha, Alex, Ryan, Michael, and Earnest. If Q3 is unusable, Sprints 22 and 23 are the baseline.
The dip. New ways of working are slower before they're faster.Continue unless the dip persists through Sprint 26; then revisit the changes, not the plan.
Holiday capacity. Sprint 26 and the regression windows around it run through the holidays, so two of the four sprints carry thinner data.The re-baseline reads the four sprints as one block against the baseline block; no single sprint decides it.
The business isn't in the room. Partners don't want to spend time on this, and their trust is the real score.Erin's interview stands in for the business in Phase 1. From Phase 3, the weekly one-line note is how they see the change, and the gate is read from Jellyfish, not from opinion.
