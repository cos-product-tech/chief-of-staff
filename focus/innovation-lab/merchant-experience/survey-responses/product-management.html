What stands out five findings from one response · the seat that owns the problem, the hypothesis, and the KPI

01 · None of these

The PM says none of the nine practices consistently hold. Including the three her role owns.

Erin is the only respondent across five surveys to check "none of these are consistently true." Not a clear problem before committing, not a hypothesis, not a KPI baseline and target, not early risk assessment, not early collaboration, not dates that reflect engineering input, not a smaller first release, not a definition of ready, not a post-release review. The three outcome statements are the Staff PM's own accountabilities, and she says they don't happen.

Q2: 0/9 · the only "none" in 10 responses
02 · Ten of ten

She is the first person to name the missing outcome read as a delay, not just an absence.

"We do not measure customer or business outcomes promptly after launch, delaying subsequent decisions" was one of her three picks. Every other respondent left that option alone while also saying the review doesn't happen. The PM sees the cost: decisions wait on evidence that is never collected. That makes the post-release gap ten of ten, with the owner of the KPI calling it a delay.

In their words Q5 · the biggest avoidable delay, and the one change

ED

Adding Passkey support - this is an ongoing initiative that has not yet been delivered, but has taken much longer than necessary to get started. This project was initiated as a part of the 'Account Takeover' effort to combat bad actors gaining access to MXM/MXC/Passport user accounts. The security team led many conversations with Key Cloak team on how the passkey management process should work, and in the end delivered a detailed, concise list of requirements. This work was then to be picked up by the 3 separate teams to complete, with Passport leading the charge due to resource availability. This is where the process fell apart. There are multiple process flows and Figma designs that are inconsistent, and only one was ever approved. Separate product managers each working from these different flows resulting in inconsistent outcomes, causing extra work for all. No one person was responsible for coordinating the work across teams and driving the collective group towards a common solution following an agreed upon schedule. Every project I have been involved in where the development spans multiple teams has been a similar experience. The gap (based on my prior experience) is the lack of a project manager that is tasked with coordinating tasks and deliveries across teams and planning for the delivery of the final product.
