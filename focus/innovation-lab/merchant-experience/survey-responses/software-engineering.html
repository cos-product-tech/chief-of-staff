What stands out six findings from five responses · the people who build it, in their own numbers

01 · Four of five

Work starts before the requirements are done.

"Requirements, acceptance criteria, or key decisions are incomplete when engineering work begins" is the top pick, four of five. And zero of five say they understand the customer problem and desired outcome before implementation begins. Two of the four verbatims are the same story: the Embedded Payments Form (EPF) MVP, where dates were set before features were discussed and the widget the work depended on wasn't deployed yet.

Q1: 4/5 · Q2: 0/5 understand the problem first · Q5: EPF named three times
02 · Dates first

One engineer describes a planning model where engineering shapes the date. Four don't.

Offered six models, Ramya picked the strongest; the other four did not. Two say "we do not have a consistent approach," one says the business proposes a date before engineering assesses feasibility, one says engineering contributes but the plan gets revised. Two of five say engineering has a meaningful role before dates are committed.

Q3: 1/5 on the top model · Q2: 2/5 engineering input before dates
03 · Mid-flight discovery

Architecture gets discovered during development, by whoever has the ticket.

Michael: technical discovery happened during development, defects were logged as the work went, and cross-team architectural dependencies were "left to the individual engineer working the ticket." Alex: a PCE library that couldn't run in Angular.js or the C# Checkout API, discovered after the work was assigned, with two I1s as the result. Both ask for the same thing: time for technical discussion before coding.

Q5: 2/4 · ties to probe 13 · Alex's two I1s are guardrail G2
04 · Four of five

Engineers own their own quality, and QA agrees.

Four of five engineers checked it: engineers test their own changes, and quality is owned throughout development rather than left to QA at the end. Two of three QA engineers said the same. Ramya, the one who didn't, is also the one who checked the planning statements the others left blank; worth asking her about both in the follow-up.

Q2: 4/5 · QA tab: 2/3 · the strongest agreement in any survey
05 · Still silent

Hypothesis, KPI, and post-release read: almost nobody.

One engineer (Ramya) sees a hypothesis on a commitment; none see a KPI with a baseline and target, and none see a review of the outcome after release. Two of five marked "metrics and outcomes of shipped work" as not applicable to their role, which says the same thing a different way.

Q2: hypothesis 1/5 · baseline 0/5 · post-release 0/5 · Q4: metrics N/A 2/5
06 · AI

AI has taken the syntax out of the bottleneck. The bottleneck moved to system design.

The most AI-adopted role so far: all five positive on understanding requirements, technical discovery, writing code, and test creation (four "faster and better" on tests). Nothing "unclear benefit." Ramya is positive on all ten activities, the only full sweep in any survey. Michael's line is the finding: "AI adoption has massively taken the classic syntax work out of the bottleneck, but system design knowledge and changes as a whole continue to be a potential bottleneck." Metrics and monitoring are the untouched corners.

Q4: 5/5 positive on 4 activities · 0 "unclear benefit" · metrics 1/5

AD

A recent example of an unclear set of requirements that was assigned to the MerchCap team (and then cascaded into other issues) was to refactor the existing address validation in both the MXM UI and Checkout API (pps; legacy .NET) so that non US/Canada postal codes would be allowed (the push for this was to support the ExpoGroup rollout as I recall). While this refactor was being discussed, there was also a push to align the MXM/Checkout address validation logic with what was implemented in the prth-address-validation library. The library originated from the PCE team and was leveraged in the new PCE Checkout widget. Long story short, there were several items that caused confusion/delays: - The prth-address-validation library was initially incompatible with Angular.js (I added a bridge of sorts during an POC). - Due to conflicting requirements, there was considerable back and forth between PR reviews. - prth-address-validation is a JavaScript based library and therefore can't be integrated into the Checkout API (C# .net). An attempt to convert portions of the library was attempted... but caused delays. - Concerns were raised that unilaterally updating the existing address validation logic might break existing customer integrations. There have since been at least two i1s raised due to postal code validation inconsistencies. It was also discussed that both MXM and the Checkout API need feature flag support (Statsig). If feature flag support was implemented, it would have been possible to enable the new address validation for only ExpoGroup, which is currently not the case (the MXM UI has partial Statsig support; Checkout API doesn't have it at all). Implementing Statsig in the Checkout API has been POC'd, and should be implemented ASAP. One change that would have delivered value sooner: An earlier alignment discussion/POC (prior to when I was asked to look into this) between the PCE and the MerchCap teams in order to verify that integrating the prth-address-validation library into MXM was actually required based on the specific needs of ExpoGroup. It's unclear if using this new library saved any time or produced a benefit for MXM (probably not).
