▾QA Engineering: Mamatha Yella and Earnest GibbsMonday, November 9, 2026 · 62 minutes · video · hosted and facilitated by Amrita, with Steve and EricAmritaSteveEric

Regression is redone almost every cycle, and MerCap is not the team causing it.

A clean MXM regression takes about a day and a half, not the five or six hours quoted earlier, once load testing and the sprint work that competes for the same people are counted. What breaks it is a hotfix landing mid-cycle: production is on S14 while S15 is in UAT, someone patches S14, the patch has to be merged back into S15, and if it touches checkout the results are reviewed again because checkout touches payments. Often the team learns the next morning that a build went out the night before. Mamatha's data point: the MerCap team has not hotfixed in three to four months. The hotfixes now come from Quan's Global and PMFP teams. Our read: MerCap's regression time is set by other teams' release discipline, which means no change inside MerCap fixes it on its own. Steve and Amrita are looking at why Global and PMFP hotfix so often.

Unannounced deployments wipe out regression already done.

Two sprints ago Avni was 60% through the manual cases when an artifact from core platform and IDC was deployed into UAT without notice. The run was void and regression restarted. Mamatha says it happens about once every three or four regressions, and she now compares the DIP artifacts against what is actually deployed every day to catch it. Her ask is modest: tell us before you deploy, so the tester can switch to other work instead of redoing hers. Our read: the cost is invisible on every board, because the work was done; it just has to be done twice. A posted regression window and a one-line notice before any UAT deployment would cost the other teams almost nothing.

Sandbox slips to the end of week two because the DIP closes late.

The intent is sandbox at the end of the first regression week. In practice the Tuesday afternoon DIP review waits on contributing teams whose builds arrive Wednesday; a Liquibase or SQL failure needs the developer who wrote it, and when that is IDC the turnaround is slow. QA is a dev environment, the data lives in UAT, and nobody owns how much data is right.

Two to three years ago all testing moved to UAT; last year it moved back to QA, but developers also deploy to QA, so QA works as a dev environment and lacks the data UAT accumulated. The automation was built for UAT and many tests only point there. UAT has the opposite problem: the load-test merchant holds about two million records, each run adds 6,000 to 12,000 payments, and payments cannot be deleted, so when response times spike the team switches merchants. Amrita's ideal of tests that create and delete their own data runs into that last fact. Earnest is moving test data to one place and making tests run in both QA and UAT. Our read: this is probe 04 confirmed from the QA seat. Test data is not a ticket-by-ticket problem; it needs a decision on what each environment is for.

MXC tests have authors but no owners.

Shift left depends on who happens to be on the team.

The ABM team, five or six developers to one QA, already practices it. On MerCap it has not been needed since Earnest joined, though Michael, Andrew, and Rama know the framework and have added tests when asked. Where we see opportunity, and how it changes the recommendations.

1. Announce before you deploy into UAT during regression. A posted regression window and a one-line notice in the quality channel before any deployment, owned by the team deploying. ▾Raimundo Puche and Lilly VakiliNinja and Director, Technical Program Management · EPMO · Wednesday, October 28, 2026 · conversation held by BartBart

The MerCap Jira backlog is past the point where it can be groomed.

Ray showed Jira statistics on the backlog: more than 200 tickets, many of them over a year old. At that size, grooming means wading through years of stale tickets to find the few that are real, so sprint planning inherits whatever is on top. It explains observations elsewhere on these pages: work arrives unready, stories go into the sprint unestimated, and spare capacity has nothing ready to pull. The age of the tickets is the part to fix first, because a ticket that is over a year old and was never prioritized is evidence that no one has decided it matters.

Next step: a Jira hygiene working session with Erin.

Bart proposes it when the team is ready, with a goal of making sprint planning and grooming more effective. To make it count, bring three things: a rule for what is stale (for example, untouched for a year gets closed or re-justified by its owner), a short list of what stays on the roadmap, and a ready-next queue of two or three estimated stories in sprint order. Success looks like a backlog small enough to read in one sitting, and a drop in unestimated or unready stories entering Sprint 23.
