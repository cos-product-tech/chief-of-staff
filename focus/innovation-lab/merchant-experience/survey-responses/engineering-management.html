What stands out four findings from one response · the director's view of his own system

01 · Three picks

Unclear outcomes, incomplete readiness, and waiting.

Quan's three delays: the problem and desired outcome are unclear; requirements, acceptance criteria, or the definition of ready are incomplete at planning; and work waits on code reviews, approvals, decisions, or other teams. Two of the three match QA's top picks exactly. He did not pick capacity, visibility, tech debt, or testing.

Q1: 3 picks · 2 overlap with QA · 1 overlaps with TPM
02 · One of nine

He knows the team's capacity. Nothing else holds.

The only statement Quan checked is "we maintain a current view of actual team capacity." Unchecked: engineering input before dates, explicit trade-offs when work arrives, reviewing delivery signals, understanding where capacity goes, a hypothesis per commitment, early collaboration, definition of ready and done, and KPI review after release. The director's self-assessment is harsher than QA's.

Q2: 1/9 · QA averaged 2–3 of 10 · TPM 8/9
03 · The model

Capacity is assessed, then overrun by unplanned work and changing priorities.

Of seven coordination models, Quan chose "we assess capacity and dependencies before commitments are made, but our forecasts are often affected by unplanned work, changing priorities, or inaccurate assumptions." That is a sprint addition rate of 263% described in words. It also explains how capacity can be known (finding 02) and still not protect the plan.

Q3 · ties to secondary measure 04, sprint addition rate
04 · AI

AI is in use for diagnosis and reporting; not yet for planning.

Faster and better for investigating delays and rework, incident and reliability analysis, and docs, meeting synthesis, and status reporting; better quality for reviewing requirements and technical context; saves time on code quality and review. Exploring on the four planning activities: capacity forecasting, Jira and Jellyfish analysis, prioritization and trade-offs, and KPIs. Nothing "not using."

How commitments are established and capacity managed
Q3 · one answer from seven, ordered strongest to weakest.
We maintain a current, evidence-based view of capacity, including existing commitments, work in progress, operational load, and dependencies. Engineering helps establish realistic delivery expectations, and new work triggers explicit trade-offs.
QWe assess capacity and dependencies before commitments are made, but our forecasts are often affected by unplanned work, changing priorities, or inaccurate assumptions.
We use historical delivery data or capacity estimates, but do not consistently account for all operational work, existing commitments, or cross-team dependencies.
Delivery dates are generally driven by business priorities or leadership expectations, with engineering capacity and feasibility assessed afterward.
We frequently add work after a sprint or commitment starts without explicitly removing or deferring existing work.
We lack reliable capacity and delivery-flow data, so commitments depend heavily on individual judgment and experience.
We do not have a consistent approach; it varies significantly across teams or initiatives.

Read: one rung below the TPM's answer. The rung is "unplanned work, changing priorities, or inaccurate assumptions." Sprint 22 should show which of the three it mostly is, and Jellyfish already says addition rate is 263%.
