What stands out six findings from three responses · small sample, read as signal to verify in Sprint 22, not as proof

01 · Unanimous

The biggest delay is upstream of QA: nobody knows what "done" looks like when work arrives.

All three picked "the customer or business problem and expected outcome are unclear before implementation and testing begin" as a top-three delay, and zero of three say requirements and acceptance criteria are consistently clear. Nobody picked regression length, automation coverage, or QA being involved too late.

Q1: 3/3 · Q2: 0/3 on clear requirements
02 · Strongest verbatim theme

Cross-team dependencies are costing whole regression days, and the survey didn't offer that as an option.

Two of three wrote about the same incident unprompted: an unresolved ACH defect caused 100+ automation failures that masked real defects until Day 5 and delayed the Sandbox release; a PCE (Commerce API) dependency forced Embedded Payments Form (EPF) tests back to manual. "At least an additional regression day" each time.

Q5: 2/3 · not in the Q1 option list · add to Sprint 22 watch list
03 · Rework

Late-changing requirements create test effort on work that never ships.

Passkey, Billing Address, and Unrecognized Device ID are named by two respondents as tickets where requirements or business decisions changed after testing started, producing rework and automation updates for changes that "may not proceed to production."

Q5: 2/3 · ties to finding 01
04 · Working

The QA machinery itself gets good marks from the people inside it.

Two of three say engineers test their own changes, critical workflows have automated coverage, and automated tests are fast and reliable. Regression results arrive in 2–5 business days, well inside the two-week regression window. The question for Sprint 22 is where the other five to eight days go.

Q2: 2/3 on four machinery statements · Q3: 2–3 days (2), 4–5 days (1)
05 · Silent

Nobody sees a hypothesis or a post-release read on whether the work worked.

Zero of three say commitments carry a hypothesis with a KPI, baseline, and target, and zero say the team evaluates outcomes after release. QA can't tell whether what it certified mattered. This is the "value" half of speed to value, and it's absent in every response.

Q2: 0/3 on both outcome statements
06 · AI

AI is real for generating tests and nowhere else yet.

All three get value from AI when generating and maintaining automated tests (two "saves time," one "faster and better"). Requirements analysis, debugging, production-defect analysis, and CI/CD are mostly "not using" or "unclear benefit." The 100% automation goal and the test-data problem are where AI help would land first.

MY

Cross-team dependencies were the biggest source of avoidable delay in our recent regression. An unresolved ACH payment defect, with delayed support from the ACH team, caused over 100 automation failures. That noise masked genuine product defects until Day 5 and delayed the Sandbox release. Last few regressions there were some defects introduced by other teams where we had to wait till the defect fix and run the automation and review results (at least cause an additional regression day). A similar dependency on the PCE (Commerce API) team left Embedded Payments Form (EPF) automation failing, with no visibility into defect status, forcing manual execution. EPF manual test execution do not need much time but just want to bring this up as we are trying to achieve 100% automation. Unclear or late-changing requirements (Passkey and Billing Address) added rework and effort on changes that may not reach production. These risks could have been identified by providing clear Acceptance Criteria. One last thing I want to bring up is any delays in regression will consume significant QA time and the focus is mostly concentrated on release delivery and this impacts the sprint work.

AA

Cross-team dependencies have been a major challenge for QA, particularly during ACH failures that caused numerous automation regression failures and delayed the Sandbox release because timely support from the ACH team was unavailable. We have also faced delays due to unclear or changing requirements and late business decisions, such as with Billing Address, Passkey, and Unrecognized Device ID tickets, sometimes resulting in rework or testing effort on changes that may not proceed to production. These risks could be addressed earlier by aligning QA, development, dependent teams, and business stakeholders on requirements, dependencies, priorities, and release readiness before implementation and automation updates begin. This would help us focus our efforts on confirmed priorities, reduce avoidable rework, and deliver value sooner without compromising quality.
