What this is — 60 seconds

01Every role has a public card

Each of your directs has a role card: five capability areas (Craft, Impact, Ownership, Influence, Judgment), each a written expectation with what evidence looks like. The card above theirs is their development plan. No folklore, no guessing.

02One card, five uses

The same card is the job description, the interview standard, the Capability Assessment line, the development plan (one card up), and the promotion criterion. Coach with the card's words and your everyday feedback matches exactly what the annual performance review measures — no translation, no surprises.

03You own four bars

Your own track's cards hold you to four bars: hiring, performance, culture, and measurement. The measurement bar in one line — individuals assessed against their card with evidence, teams measured with paired delivery data, outcomes above everything.

A suggested path, at your own pace

1
Read your own card first. Find your level on Engineering Management (or QAE Management), then read the card one level up. You'll coach this system more honestly once you've felt it point at you.
2
Read each direct's card — and the one above it. For every report: their level's card, then the next one up. Note one clause per person where the next card already fairly describes them, and one where it clearly doesn't yet. That's your first coaching map.
3
Start an evidence note per person. One running doc each — copy the Evidence Note template; its tables feed the promotion packet row-for-row. Every time you see card-level evidence — an ADR with an afterlife, a trend with the fix named, an incident class that stopped happening, a person they grew — one line with a date. Nobody remembers the year's early months when the annual performance review arrives; this note is what makes it honest instead of recency-biased.
4
Introduce the Engineering Expectations in your next 1:1s. Use the script below — and for the full walk, use the Expectations Playbook: eight plays, each with the point, the page to open, and the why, word-for-word ready. The goal of conversation one is not assessment — it's orientation: they read their card, they read up, you both point at the same words.
5
Open your team's signals. Per the measurement stack: outcomes your team exists to move, paired delivery-and-quality signals beneath them, activity as diagnostics only. Find the one number that improved recently — and check whether its outcome moved with it. That reflex is the whole measurement duty. The Jellyfish Playbook shows you exactly which pages to open, in which meeting, asking which questions.
The first-1:1 script — say it in your own voice:
"Our Engineering Expectations are public — every expectation for every level, including mine, is written down and open. Before our next 1:1, read your role card and the one above it. I'll do the same. Then let's compare notes: where does the next card already describe you, and where doesn't it yet? That gap is what we'll build your development around — and from now on, when I give you feedback, I'll tie it to the card's words so nothing I say is ever a surprise in a review."
Then leave them with the Expectations Playbook — the whole framework as eight plays they can walk themselves in fifteen minutes, and re-open any time.

Your operating rhythm — where the Expectations live in your calendar

CadenceThe habitWhy it compounds
1:1Coach in card language. Praise and correction both point at a clause ("that design doc got used, not filed — that's the Senior Craft expectation"). Capture one evidence line per meaningful observation. When a gap needs a chance to close, create the opportunity — nobody is scored down for chances never given, so unearned gaps are your debt, not theirs.Twelve months of 1:1s becomes a finished Capability Assessment. Zero archaeology when the annual performance review arrives.
DAILYA quick Jellyfish scan, most days. Open Team Summary for key metrics and unusual trends, scan Deliverables for blocked or idle work, and run the hygiene sweep — stuck tickets, unlinked PRs, missing due dates.Blocked work found today costs a conversation; found at sprint review, it costs the sprint. Daily reads build the baseline that makes real anomalies jump out — and clean data is what makes your measurement bar trustworthy at all.
SPRINT / WEEKLYMisses examined with the data open. Delivery signals read in pairs — speed never counts unless its quality pair held. A number that improved while its outcome didn't is a flag, not a win. Make Jellyfish a real weekly habit — the measurement duty isn't held from memory.Your team learns metrics are questions, not quotas — which is what keeps them honest.
MONTHLYSelf-audit the four bars: Did every hire this period raise the level? Any performance conversation I'm avoiding? Would my team describe debate as safe and honest? Am I measuring outcomes or admiring activity?The avoided conversation is the expensive one. This catches it at week four, not month nine.
QUARTERLYInvestment and maturity — the quarter's five moves:
1Investment allocations in Jellyfish reviewed against the product engineering operating model — evidenced, not asserted.
2Run the Team EMM (Team Engineering Maturity Model) — you, your senior engineer, and your quality partner, evidence-anchored — and give its improvement bet real roadmap space.
3Refresh each evidence note's Assessment tab: run the quarterly Below / At / Above read across the five card areas, and update the computed delivery record — so the Q1 review is assembly, not authorship.
4Check each person's opportunity record: does everyone have next-level work in flight?
5Raise promotion readiness at calibration wherever the evidence is sustained — it convenes quarterly.
Promotion cases are built from opportunities you created quarters earlier — or they aren't built at all.
ANNUALLYThe annual performance review period: Capability Assessments against each card — card reads per area, official rating on the company scale — and promotion packets for anyone already operating at the next level with their committed business outcomes delivered. If you ran the rhythm above, this is assembly, not authorship.A promotion case should surprise nobody who read your evidence notes. That's the test you pass here.

The plays — when the moment arrives

"I think I'm ready for promotion."
Open the target card together, block by block: "show me the sustained evidence for each" — and the business outcomes they and the team committed to: did they land? Gaps → create the opportunities, note them. Evidence there → start the promotion packet; readiness can be raised at any quarterly calibration. Either way, they leave with a map, not a mood. A "not yet" with a gap map is a route, never a rejection.
Underperformance you can finally name.
The card's first sentence is the anchor: is it a fair description of their sustained behavior, given the opportunities they actually had? Have the conversation early, kind and direct, with specifics and a follow-up date — per Talent Processes — When an area is Below. Avoiding it is choosing the team's decline.
You're hiring.
The standard you hold inside is the standard you hire against. Build the posting from your track's JD recipe, get certified via the Interviewer Certification Training, and run the published loop. Your interview slot is the most important meeting of your day.
"I want to try management" / "I miss building."
Both are sanctioned, level-for-level moves in Career Mobility — management is a change of craft, not a promotion, and Team Lead is a two-way door by design. Moves land with the annual performance review period; walk the destination card together first.
A strong engineer on a struggling team.
Assess the person against their card, full stop — the Team EMM measures the team and is never evidence about a person, per the firewalls. A strong engineer in a weak system is exactly who this framework must recognize.
Your expert is a single point of failure.
This is yours to close, actively: work the knowledge layer — docs, runbooks, recorded walkthroughs, shadow ownership, deliberate rotation, AI-ingestable stores. Success test: someone new to the scope can understand, debug, and contribute with less SME dependency — and no part of the scope depends on a single head.

The mirror — growing into your own role

Every rule above points at you too.

Your card sits on the same public page as everyone else's, your manager assesses you against it in the same annual performance review period, and the card one level up is your development plan — reading it is the assignment. The management track's standing test: the evidence is what your team does because of you, not what you do personally — and the single-point-of-failure duty includes you: name your own SPOFs and close them. If reading your own card stings a little somewhere, that clause is your next quarter. Start there, out loud, with your own manager: modeling the gap conversation is the single fastest way to make your directs trust it.

Three things that break trust — never do these

1. Never use Team EMM results for or against a person — the firewall is absolute.  2. Never bend a card to fit a promotion you want — the standard is the card, not your advocacy; calibration exists to catch exactly this.  3. Never save feedback for the review — if anything in the annual performance review is a surprise, the system didn't fail; the rhythm above wasn't run.
